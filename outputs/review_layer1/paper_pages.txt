Home-Win Prediction in International Football:
Team Strength, Historical Information, and Regional Variation
Names, student IDs, and classes to be confirmed
Abstract
Historical home-win rates in international football may reflect differences in team strength
and match composition as well as venue-related conditions. We investigate how much these
patterns contribute to pre-match prediction. A cohort of 24,917 non-neutral matches from
1980–2024 supports comparisons across eras, match types, and regions. A dynamic model
learns strength from earlier results, including eligible neutral matches; a joint model then
combines the strength distribution with match context. Retrospective chronological eval-
uation on 21,413 matches from 1990–2024 compares background-only, strength-only, and
joint predictions. The joint model reduces log loss by 16.12% relative to background alone
and 0.42% relative to strength alone, with 70.01% binary accuracy for home-win/non-home-
win decisions. Historical-window comparisons identify a trade-off between fitting cost and
probability quality, without establishing a universally best length. Regional checks reveal
uneven robustness and probability bias. A sealed release covers 52 future fixtures, with 49
main predictions and three withheld under the venue rule; outcomes remain unevaluated.
Strength supplies most of the predictive gain, while context adds a modest increment whose
interpretation is limited by historical reuse and model assumptions.
Keywords: international football; pre-match strength; home-win probability; probabilistic pre-
diction
1
Introduction
1.1
Motivation: From Historical Rates to Pre-Match Decisions
An international team’s historical home-win rate is easy to calculate but diﬀicult to interpret.
A high rate may reflect strong home teams, weak opponents, or the competitions and periods
represented. Comparing regions or decades without accounting for these differences can confuse
changes in the schedule with changes in performance. For an upcoming match, the practical
question is how much these historical patterns help once both teams’ recent performances are
known.
Description identifies where home-win rates differ; prediction asks whether those differences
help assign probabilities to future outcomes.
A group difference may add little information
once team strength is considered, while a plausible overall average can conceal poor individual
predictions. National-team records also involve irregular match intervals, changing identities,
neutral venues, and uneven opponent combinations. These features require a documented sample
and clear rules about which information is available before each prediction.
We study home-win probability, combining draws and away wins as the alternative outcome.
Here, context means the era, match type, and geographic groups of both teams. We assess what
strength and context contribute to prediction. The design supports historical and model-based
comparisons, but does not identify the causal effect of relocating the same match from a neutral
venue to a home ground.

1.2
Related Work and Study Positioning
Pollard and Armatas [1] examined home advantage in the group stages of qualification for the
2006, 2010, and 2014 World Cups. Their competition-specific setting provides background for
our broader comparison of eras, friendlies, and geographic groups. Our home-win percentages
describe a different target and sample from other measures of home advantage.
Ratings turn past results into measures of current strength. Hvattum and Arntzen [2] used
Elo-derived covariates in ordered-logit football prediction models. Glickman [3] developed dy-
namic paired comparisons that represent uncertainty about changing ability. Dixon and Coles [4]
modelled English league and cup scores with a dynamic Poisson-regression approach. These
studies motivate distinguishing how past results inform strength from how strength is converted
into an outcome probability.
Proper scoring rules provide a basis for evaluating probabilities beyond counting correctly
predicted outcomes [5].
Repeated team appearances also motivate attention to dependence
between matches [6]. We use these ideas to compare models on common samples and to assess
uncertainty under explicit assumptions. Our implementations and their limits are set out in the
Method section.
1.3
Research Questions, Contributions, and Roadmap
We organize the study around three questions:
1. RQ1: How do historical home-win rates differ across eras, match types, and regions, and
how far do composition adjustment and uncertainty assessment support their interpreta-
tion?
2. RQ2: How much predictive information does pre-match strength supply, and how much
does match context add once strength is included?
3. RQ3: How do historical information length and geographic setting affect predictive per-
formance, computational cost, and the conditions for using the model?
The contribution is a traceable analysis linking historical comparisons, strength-informed
prediction, and practical use. We compare background-only, strength-only, and joint predic-
tions on the same matches, examine information length and regional applicability, and issue a
sealed future prediction set. Strength carries the main predictive gain, context adds a smaller
increment, and the evidence does not establish a universally best historical window or equal
regional applicability.
Section 2 presents the data, models, and evaluation protocol. Section 3 reports the historical
comparisons, predictive evidence, and prospective application. The discussion draws together
their implications and proposed extensions. Future outcomes are outside the present evaluation.
2
Method
2.1
Study Design and Data
We used the International Football Results dataset maintained by martj42 [7]. The registered
snapshot contains 49,945 matches dated 30 November 1872–6 October 2026, recording dates,
teams, scores, tournaments, locations, and neutral-venue status. It is licensed under CC0 and
identified by SHA-256; the upstream commit and original acquisition time are unknown. Scores
include extra time where recorded and exclude penalty-shootout goals. The main outcome is
Yi = 1 for a home win and 0 for a draw or away win. A nominal home-team label at a neutral
venue does not establish home advantage.

Preparation preserves the originals and records each row’s disposition. Audits validate dates,
integer scores, and venue flags, and flag duplicate candidates, conflicting scores, and reversed
pairings. Critical unresolved records are quarantined; optional missing fields restrict eligibility
for particular analyses.
At the 31 December 2024 preparation cutoff, 48,102 matches were
included, 1,803 were outside the window, and 40 were isolated. Identity and eligibility review
then retained 24,917 of 26,055 study candidates, excluding 1,138. This includes 117 matches
accepted following the project owner’s manual verification, without a new independent oﬀicial-
source audit. Team identifiers denote football entities, which need not be sovereign countries;
historical names and administrative changes are handled through explicit registry decisions.
The comparative cohort covers non-neutral matches in 1980–2024; earlier eligible records
initialize strength.
Eras are 1980–1989, 1990–1999, 2000–2009, 2010–2019, and the shorter
2020–2024 period. Normalized Friendly identifies friendlies; all other retained tournaments
form the non-friendly group. Geographic labels begin with UN M49 [8], with study overrides in
both team roles: Turkey is in Europe/Southern Europe, Iran in Western Asia, and Australia in
Asia/South-eastern Asia. Africa uses North, West, and remaining sub-Saharan groups; Oceania
is not subdivided. These are fixed research labels, not historical confederation memberships.
Every match on a date is predicted before any result from that date updates strength,
because reliable intraday order is unavailable. Outcome-model coeﬀicients are refitted annually
using previous years only. Strength learning uses 38,919 eligible matches from 1950: 5,581 pre-
1980 matches, 8,421 later neutral matches, and the 24,917-match comparative cohort. Initial
development and selection used 1970–1974 and 1975–1979, respectively, but later diagnostics
and revisions reused subsequent history. Evaluation is therefore retrospective. Figure 1 shows
the overlapping learning and comparison samples.

Conditional contrasts require additional validation because outcomes update the strength

of all match pairs. Follow-up simulations use a preallocated sequential error budget, following
time-uniform control principles [10]. This validation concerns conditional contrasts under those
generators, separately from predictive loss comparisons.
History-window comparisons vary strength memory and outcome-learning history separately.
Finite-memory states are reconstructed annually; an adaptive choice uses past performance only.
Outcome parameters stay fixed within each evaluation year while strength updates from earlier
actual results. Timings distinguish the measured outcome-fitting component from total costs.
Regional diagnostics hold predictions fixed, standardize nine cells combining strength-gap and
strength-uncertainty categories, and use cutpoints learned before 2010. Leave-one-year/team
checks omit affected evaluation matches, in either team role, without rebuilding the learning
history.

3.2
Strength versus Context
For RQ2, background-only, strength-only, and joint predictors were evaluated on the same
21,413 matches from 1990–2024, each using earlier information. This is a retrospective chrono-
logical comparison: the archive also informed development and selection, as described in Sec-
tion 2.1.
Strength supplied most of the improvement (Table 3; Figure 3). Mean log loss declined from
0.671936 for background alone to 0.566020 for strength alone and 0.563630 for the joint predictor.
The joint model’s relative reductions were 16.12% against background alone and 0.42% against
strength alone.
Binary accuracy followed the same ordering: 58.19%, 69.83%, and 70.01%.
These are home-win/non-home-win accuracies; relative loss reductions are a separate measure.
Adding context also reduced the Brier score from 0.193698 to 0.192657 and mean bias from
+0.9308 to +0.2398 pp. Yet background-only had the smallest absolute mean bias while produc-
ing the worst probability scores. Matching the overall frequency can therefore conceal weak in-
dividual predictions; bias alone does not establish calibration across probability levels or groups.
The separate conditional analysis uses 24,917 matches and a fixed reference. Its strength-
adjusted friendly-minus-other contrast was −3.4957 pp, with a range from −7.6712 to 0.6797 pp.
The paired change after adding strength was +0.3624 pp, ranging from −2.8619 to 3.5868 pp.

3.3
Historical Information and Computational Trade-offs
For RQ3, we varied the history used to reconstruct strength separately from the history
used to estimate outcome probabilities. Evaluation covered 16,599 matches in 2000–2024, with
9,727 in the main 2010–2024 comparison. Among six tested combinations, cumulative history
at both stages gave the lowest main-comparison log loss, 0.559135 (Figure 4). Annual selection
using past performance gave 0.560159. We therefore retain the cumulative default within these
choices. This does not imply equal influence for every old match: the dynamic strength process
allows ability to change.
A 12-year outcome-learning window, with cumulative strength estimation fixed, increased
loss from 0.559135 to 0.561131, or 0.3571% using unrounded values. In the controlled timing
batch, this component took 105.29 rather than 312.58 seconds, a 66.31% saving. This quantifies
a cost–prediction trade-off for that component.

4
Discussion and Conclusion
4.1
Answers to the Research Questions and Contributions
For RQ1, historical rates identify patterns that require explanation. Era adjustment reduces
the friendly–other-match gap, and geographic comparisons depend on the opponents represented.
Several conditional ranges remain too broad to resolve a direction.
These results establish
comparable observations for the strength-based analysis, while leaving pure home effects and
universal regional rankings unidentified.
For RQ2, pre-match strength supplies most of the predictive information, with a smaller
contribution from context. A high historical group rate can therefore add little once both teams’
strengths are considered. Our approach shares the motivation of dynamic paired comparisons [3],
with project-specific choices for jumps, information weights, and the outcome model. Its value
is demonstrated against our internal predictors; comparisons with FIFA rankings, Elo, or other
external systems remain unperformed.
For RQ3, cumulative history remains the default among the tested choices.
A shorter
outcome-fitting window offers a practical saving with a small predictive cost. Regional diag-
nostics qualify the aggregate gain: Africa’s contextual increment is sensitive to particular years
or teams, and opposite biases remain in selected uncertainty groups. These findings identify
where further testing and calibration would be useful.
12


========== [PDF PAGE 13] ==========
Together, the stages connect historical observations to a forecast with documented infor-
mation dates and an explicit withholding rule.
Probability scores [5] reveal differences that
average bias or accuracy alone can miss. The sealed release makes that connection concrete and
preserves predictions for later evaluation.
4.2
Limitations and Lessons Learned
The evidence applies to the registered population. Some historical identities and membership
decisions rely on owner-accepted records, and the global archive has not been independently ver-
ified in full. Geographic overrides are research definitions, while sparse opponent combinations
limit comparability. Scores can include extra time, so the target cannot uniformly be described
as regulation-time results.
Estimated strength, approximate filtering, jump assumptions, and restricted context terms
may leave important variation unexplained. Conditional comparisons do not account for all
differences in travel, team selection, venues, or match importance. The main model also com-
bines draws and away wins, unlike the auxiliary three-class model; their accuracies are not
interchangeable.
Chronological prediction excludes each match’s own outcome from its inputs, but historical
development and selection reuse the archive. It is not an untouched final test set. Simulated
validation establishes results under specified generators, and no real-data dependence-adjusted
interval establishes persistence of the small contextual gain. Deletion checks diagnose evaluation
composition without rebuilding the learning history.
Consequently, prospective testing and
regional diagnostics remain necessary to assess wider use.
The historical uncertainty model also retains a documented failure boundary under perma-
nent global shocks. This reinforces the restriction of its envelopes to the admitted generating
mechanisms; broad interval results cannot be treated as guarantees for arbitrary football histo-
ries.
4.3
Future Work
Four proposed extensions address these gaps. First, the joint layer could estimate home-
win, draw, and away-win probabilities together. A draw interval, as used in TrueSkill [14], is
one starting point. Another model could instead divide the non-home-win probability between
draws and away wins. Both require three-class evaluation against the existing auxiliary model.
Second, calibration could address systematic probability bias using mappings learned from
earlier predictions and subsequently observed outcomes. Temperature scaling provides a method-
ological example [15], although its published evidence concerns neural classifiers. Benefits here
require chronological and regional evaluation; improved calibration need not increase classifica-
tion accuracy.
Third, goal-based models could provide richer outcomes, following the direction illustrated
by Dixon and Coles [4]. Score definitions must be checked, and results and their underlying goals
must not be counted as independent evidence. Reliable venue or pre-match squad information
is another candidate, provided availability at the prediction date is recorded. These inputs have
not been tested as explanations of the present biases.
Fourth, club transfer would require new ratings and parameters to handle seasons, transfers,
promotion and relegation, and links between leagues. A single-league study with explicit season
transitions would be a feasible first test of the reusable evaluation framework.
These four
extensions remain proposals rather than demonstrated improvements.
4.4
Conclusion
Historical home-win patterns become useful for prediction when considered alongside pre-
match strength. Strength carries most of the observed gain; context adds a modest increment
